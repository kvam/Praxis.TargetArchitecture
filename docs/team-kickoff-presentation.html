One source of truth, so the contract cannot drift silently.

  • Every shared shape starts in the backend. A type the frontend needs is a backend change first, not a TypeScript interface someone types out to unblock themselves.
  • Generated files are output, not source. Editing one is like editing a build artifact: it works until the next regeneration, then it silently doesn't.
  • A contract change is one change, not two tickets. Whoever changes the DTO ships the frontend that consumes it, in the same piece of work.
  • Most frontend and backend friction simply never happens. It comes from two hand-maintained copies of the truth slowly disagreeing. With one copy there is nothing to reconcile, and nothing to call a meeting about.

The one exception to slice independence

Only one slice may be called: Get

Slices do not call each other. If they did, we would be back to a tangle of services with no boundary. There is exactly one dependency we allow, because the alternative is worse.

Write slice
Create / Update
Does the work, then returns nothing but the identifier of the row it touched.
→
Read slice, reused
Get service
The controller hands it the identifier and asks for the canonical shape.
→
Response
The same DTO as GET
A created resource looks exactly like a fetched one, because it is the same code.
  • The write service returns an identifier, not a DTO. Its job is the change. Describing the result is the read slice's job, and it already does it.
  • The dependency only ever points one way. A write controller may reference the Get service. Get never references a write slice, and no other pair of slices may reference each other at all.
  • One definition of the response shape. Without this, create and update grow their own projections, and three slices drift into three slightly different ideas of what a thing looks like.
  • If the response needs a new field, change the Get slice. That is the whole point: there is one place to change, and every endpoint that returns the resource picks it up.
Integrations

Keep the outside world at arm's length

Everything so far assumes we can change both sides of a contract and deploy them together. The moment something outside this team is involved, that stops being true, and the rules have to change with it.

Our API, for our frontend

Deployed together, generated models, changed freely. This is the one place a contract can be reshaped in a single commit.

A separate API for anyone else

External callers get their own endpoints and their own DTOs, versioned and deprecated on purpose. Never our internal shapes, or every rename becomes someone else's outage.

Their model stops at the edge

Whatever a third party sends, we translate it into our own types in the integration slice. Their field names and their quirks do not travel any further in.

  • Outbox on the way out. Write the message in the same transaction as the state change, and publish after it commits. Otherwise you eventually get a change nobody was told about, or an announcement of something that rolled back.
  • Inbox on the way in. Record the message identifier before doing the work, so a redelivery is a no-op. Every queue is at-least-once, so handlers have to be safe to run twice.
  • Their failures become our exceptions. Timeouts, partial success and retries stay inside the integration slice. The rest of the system sees a result or a domain exception, never a raw transport error.
Part two

The hard part

Usually filed under soft skills, which undersells it. The code rules keep the codebase simple. These keep us simple to work with, and they are the ones that actually decide how fast we go.

How we're organized

No frontend team. No backend team.

Full-stack by default

A feature is delivered end to end. Whoever changes a DTO changes the frontend that consumes it. Splitting work at the contract turns one task into two queues, and the handoff is where the misunderstanding lives.

Depth is welcome

It's fine, expected even, to be an expert in one half. Depth is valuable and nobody is asked to be equally strong everywhere. What isn't optional is learning enough of the other half to read, change, and review it.

We own the seam

The boundary between frontend and backend belongs to whoever is building the feature, not to a team on either side of it. Nobody optimizes their own layer at the expense of the join, because nobody has a layer to defend.

Learning the other half is the job

Not overhead to do on your own time. It repays itself the first time a change doesn't have to wait for someone else.

Full stack, better code

Owning both ends changes what you build

You expose only what the screen needs

When you also write the consumer, the speculative fields, the "might be useful later" flags and the catch-all endpoint never get invented. Seeing the real usage pattern also shows you the states and branches nobody needs, which a single-layer view never reveals.

Logic lands on the side that owns it

Filtering, sorting and totals go where the data already is, instead of being shipped across the wire and redone in the browser. You can see both costs at once, so the decision stops being a preference and becomes arithmetic.

Round trips collapse

One person building both halves notices that the screen needs three calls to render, and fixes it by changing the endpoint rather than by adding a third call.

There is nobody to defend against

Optional fields, tolerant parsing and validation duplicated on both sides are usually insurance against a person you have to negotiate with, not against a failure that can happen. Writing both ends, you design for what the system does rather than for what someone else might do to it.

You understand the problem, not your slice of it

Building the endpoint and the screen that calls it shows you how a feature is actually used, not how it looked on paper. The edge case that only appears once the UI runs against real data is exactly the one worth handling carefully, and you meet it before a user does.

Boring versus fancy becomes a real trade-off

You feel the full cost of an abstraction, the backend code and the frontend code it has to serve. That makes "obvious beats short" a judgment you can actually make instead of a slogan.

Full stack, better developer

It's not just good for the code

More gets done

No waiting on someone else's queue to unblock a feature, no re-loading context after a handoff. One person carrying a change end to end is simply faster than two people relaying it.

You can be handed anything

Nobody has to work out which half a task belongs to before giving it to you. Work that has to be routed around a person is work that waits. Being the person it never has to be routed around is quietly one of the most valuable things to be.

People want you on their work

Not "I'll take the API, you take the screen, let's sync Thursday". Picking something up means it's actually handled. Less left over for everyone else is the most reliable way there is to build trust, and trust is what buys autonomy later.

You make other people better

You can unblock a teammate stuck in your strong half, review their PR with real understanding instead of rubber-stamping it, and hold both sides of a design conversation.

Learning happens on the job

The work itself is the training. Every feature that crosses the boundary teaches you a bit more of the half you're weaker in, with no separate ramp-up project required.

Better estimates, less surprise

Knowing the true cost of a change, both layers and not just your favorite one, makes your own planning honest, and makes the whole team's forecasts more trustworthy.

Shorter outages

When something breaks in production, you already know where to look, with no relay race through two specialists to find the seam that failed.

Career resilience

A skillset tied to one layer ages with that layer's fashions. Depth plus range is worth more, and for longer, to this team and to the next one.

How we're organized

Review outside your specialty

If you can't follow code in your weaker half, that's evidence about the code, not about you.

  • Everyone reviews everything, not just their own layer. A backend person reads the frontend PR; a frontend person reads the backend PR.
  • Code that only the author can review is a bottleneck disguised as expertise.
  • Experts teach rather than gatekeep. A part of the system only one person can touch is a risk, not a strength. Explain it, pair on it, write it down.
  • Simplify until a reasonably experienced newcomer can follow it. That audience is the one-year bar in practice, and it's who we're writing for.
Team size

Ten is the ceiling, not the target

Ten is roughly the largest group that can still hold shared context without inventing process to replace it. It is a ceiling worth defending. Past it, the coordination cost quietly eats the capacity you just added.

  • Ten costs 45 connections. Twenty costs 190. Double the people, four times the coordination, and every one of those links is someone who has to be told, asked, or kept in sync.
  • Growth doesn't add capacity, it adds coordination. Each new person has to get context from everyone who already has it, paid for out of the day of the people already productive.
  • When a team stops feeling like one team, split it, don't grow it. Two teams that each own something end to end beat one larger team held together by process.
  • Process is what fills in for trust. Most sign-offs stand in for a conversation that stopped scaling. Under the ceiling that conversation is still possible, so have it.
How we're organized

Autonomy & shared ownership

  • Autonomy over approval: ship the change, let review catch what it catches.
  • Shared ownership: anyone may change any part of the system. Personal territory turns a person into a queue.
  • When we must grow, we split around whole vertical features, never around layers. Splitting by layer manufactures exactly the handoffs this architecture exists to avoid.

Flow

Limit work in progress

Everything in flight is unfinished inventory. It has cost time and delivered nothing.

  • Started is not delivered. Three tasks at ninety percent are worth exactly nothing.
  • Finish before you start. When new work arrives, the first question is what gets completed or dropped to make room for it.
  • Reviewing beats starting. Work waiting for review is already paid for and not yet earning, so clearing someone else's queue beats opening a new task of your own.
  • Small changes merge fast. Size a change to be reviewed in one sitting. Long-lived branches are work in progress too: they collect conflicts and hide risk until the worst possible moment.
  • Blocked work gets made visible, not stacked. Starting a third task to look busy turns one delay into three.

Before writing code

Doing nothing beats doing the wrong thing

Not acting is always on the table. A wrong change costs the work, the revert, and everything built on top of it in between. Waiting costs only time.

  • Understand before you act. Being busy is not progress. If the problem isn't clear yet, understanding it is the work.
  • The best code is the code you never wrote. Every line is reviewed, tested, maintained, and eventually migrated.
  • Wait for the second example. Build for what exists. An abstraction invented for one case is a guess, usually wrong in an expensive way.
  • Prefer the reversible decision. Easy to undo: decide and move on. Hard to undo: slow down. Those are the only choices that earn the delay.
  • Deleting is a contribution. Subtraction makes every future change cheaper.
How we decide

Ask what it costs to undo

Before arguing about the answer, agree on which kind of decision it is. What it costs to reverse, not how big or how interesting it is, decides how much care it has earned.

How we help each other

Pair to move a problem earlier

A problem costs almost nothing while the code is still being written, and more every hour after that. Pairing is not about company. It is about catching the thing on the first line instead of the third day.

Caught while it's being written One sentence out loud.
Caught in review A round trip, and two people rebuilding the context.
Caught after it's merged A second branch, a second review, work already built on top.
Caught in production An outage, and everything else stops until it's fixed.
Pair on this
  • A bug nobody understands yet, because two people guess faster than one person stares.
  • Anything hard to undo. Deciding together costs an hour; deciding alone can cost a migration.
  • Work in the half of the stack you are weaker in. This is how the team stops having specialists nobody can cover for.
  • The first slice of anything new, so the shape gets agreed once rather than argued later.
Do it alone
  • Work you have done twenty times. A review afterwards is enough.
  • Reading, digging, reproducing. Thinking is quieter alone.
  • All day, every day. Pairing costs more attention than it returns if it never stops.
  • When it is really supervision. If one person watches another work, that is not pairing.

When you do pair, the less experienced person types. The one at the keyboard is the one learning.

Two rules that look like they fight

Interrupting someone is cheap. High WIP makes it expensive.

We interrupt people the moment we are stuck—that is cheap. But it becomes expensive when they are juggling four unfinished tasks. The antidote is not isolation; it is finishing work.

Whose project is it

Someone else pays for every choice we make

Curiosity is the job. Trying things is how anyone gets better. But a customer system is not where we go to find out whether we like a technology, and the person who inherits our decision is rarely the person who made it.

The honest question to ask yourself

Would I still pick this if I had to support it for five years, and if nobody would ever know I had chosen it? If the answer changes once you remove the audience, it was never a technical decision.

  • Learn on the job, not with the job. There is plenty to get better at inside boring choices: the domain, the tests, the flow, the review. Experiments belong in a spike, a side project, or a deliberate and time-boxed trial we agreed on together.
  • Say the real reason out loud. "I want to try this" is a completely legitimate thing to want, and it is a discussion the team can have. It just cannot arrive disguised as a technical argument in a pull request.
Up for discussion, 1 of 4: how code reads

Boring, obvious, consistent

The next four slides are starting positions, not commandments. Each one exists so the next person to open a file, possibly you a year from now, does not have to reconstruct what the author was thinking. Argue with any of them today, and we keep what survives the argument.

No else

Guard clauses and early returns. The happy path stays flat and reads top to bottom.

Avoid try/catch

Only when the failure is genuinely handled here, never to log-and-rethrow, swallow, or return a default.

200 lines per file

Past that it is usually two ideas sharing a name. Split along the seam, not at the line count.

No abbreviations

architecturePrincipleId, not apId. Nobody has ever been slowed down by a name that was too clear.

Boring beats clever

Prefer explicit code over an abstraction that saves a few lines. Code is written once and read for years.

Match the surrounding code

Consistency beats personal preference. A file should not reveal how many people have edited it.

Comments explain why

Never what. Delete comments that restate the code. They rot, and then they lie.

Name our own types

PraxisDbContext, not AppDbContext. The prefix says this one is ours, so nobody hunts for framework docs.

Prefix our own platform types

PraxisDbContext, not AppDbContext. The prefix tells the reader this one is ours, not a framework type to go looking up.

A new endpoint is a new folder

Never a second action bolted onto an existing slice. Deleting the folder deletes the feature.

Wait for the second example

An abstraction invented for one case fits exactly one case. Duplication is cheaper than the wrong shape.

Deleting is a contribution

Up for discussion, 2 of 4: hygiene

Habits that keep production boring

Hygiene, not cleverness. Every one of these passes on your machine; skip one and nothing happens until the table is big, the request is concurrent, the environment is different, or the row has already been written, and by then it is not a code change any more.

Relationships go both ways

The child keeps FooId and Foo; the parent keeps List<Bar>. The model reads the same from either end.

The DbContext is the repository

EF already implements repository and unit of work. A wrapper over it only hides what it actually does.

Queries live in the slice

No shared query layer. Project straight into the DTO with .Select(...) so the database returns only what is used.

Reads do not track

AsNoTracking() on every read path, and loading is explicit, with no lazy proxies quietly issuing queries.

No queries inside loops

Fetch what you need in one round trip. An N+1 is invisible on your machine and brutal in production.

Everything translates to SQL

Filter, sort and page in the database. Falling back to memory works until the table grows.

One SaveChanges per request

A unit of work applies atomically, or not at all. Half-applied writes are the worst bug to find.

Never set audit fields by hand

SaveChanges owns them. Hand-written timestamps are the ones you stop trusting first.

Never renumber a persisted enum

The values are stored as integers. Reordering them silently rewrites what every existing row meant.

The DbContext is scoped

Never captured in a singleton, a static, or a parallel task. It is not thread-safe, and the bug it causes looks like anything but that.

Secrets never enter the repository

User secrets locally, the platform in production. A committed secret is leaked permanently, not temporarily.

Configuration fails at startup

Missing required settings stop the application immediately, instead of surfacing as a null at two in the morning.

Up for discussion, 3 of 4: the shape of a feature

Where each piece of a change belongs

Most arguments about structure are really arguments about where something goes. Deciding it once, out loud, means nobody has to relitigate it in a review six months from now.

Controllers stay thin

Validate the input, call the service, return. A controller with a branch in it has started keeping a secret.

One slice, one action

One controller, one public action, one service method. A second entry point means a second slice.

Slices do not call slices

The single exception: a write controller may call its own Get service, so the read shape is defined once.

No shared Helpers folder

No shared Dtos, Models or Services bucket either. Shared folders are where coupling goes to hide.

No interface for one implementation

An interface with a single implementor is a rename waiting to happen, plus a file you have to open twice.

Every DTO property is required

Optionality is expressed by the type, string?, never by leaving a field out of the payload.

DTOs are flat

Never expose an entity over the wire. The shape the frontend sees is a decision, not a side effect of the database.

Throw domain exceptions

The middleware maps them to status codes. A slice should never be picking HTTP numbers.

The backend owns the contract

If the frontend needs a different shape, change the DTO and regenerate. Never paper over it with a hand-written interface.

Generated files are output

Never hand-edited, never partially trusted. If the generated model is wrong, the backend is wrong.

Query keys come from the enum

Never an inline string, and every mutation invalidates what it affected. Stale screens are a caching bug, not bad luck.

Test against an in-memory database

Use an in-memory DbContext, never a mocked one. Cover the happy path and every domain exception you throw.

Up for discussion, 4 of 4: how much language to use

ASP.NET, EF Core and the BCL are already full of generics, reflection and abstract types, so that our code does not have to be. That is the trade: the framework absorbs the hard machinery, and what we write stays something you can read top to bottom.

First weeks

What we build first

  • Set up the scaffolding: vertical-slice backend, generated-model frontend, the reference slice as a living example.
  • Ship the smallest vertical slice end to end: one endpoint, one screen, one team member doing both halves.
  • Wire up the mechanical bar: dotnet build, dotnet test, npm run lint, npm run typecheck green before anything else counts.
  • Seed the AI configuration: instructions and skills so every contributor, human or agent, follows the same standard automatically.
  • Deploy on day one: a boring pipeline that ships small changes constantly, before there's anything exciting to ship.
Recommended reading: flow & delivery

Where these ideas come from

Goldratt
The Goal

The origin of it all: a bottleneck sets the pace of the whole system. Speeding up anything else is wasted effort. This is why we protect flow over local busyness.

Kim, Behr, Spafford
The Phoenix Project

The Goal, retold as an IT crisis novel. WIP limits, handoffs, and unplanned work made vivid, and the case for everything in our "flow of work" slides.

Gene Kim
The Unicorn Project

Same story, developer's side. Makes the case for full-stack ownership and fast feedback loops directly from the trenches.

Forsgren, Humble, Kim
Accelerate

The research behind the novels: small batches, fast feedback, and trunk-based flow are measurably correlated with elite performance, not just a nice story.

Mik Kersten
Project to Product

Why measuring flow, not utilization, is what tells you whether the team is actually delivering value.

Fred Brooks
The Mythical Man-Month

The classic case for small teams: communication paths grow faster than headcount, so adding people to a late project makes it later.

Recommended reading: team & craft

How to work together, and write it well

Woody Zuill
Mob Programming

The opposite of a silo taken to its logical end: the whole team, one keyboard, one problem, at once. Makes the case for shared ownership without a single handoff.

L. David Marquet
Turn the Ship Around!

Autonomy and shared ownership in practice: "I intend to…" instead of asking permission. The playbook behind our "autonomy over approval" rule.

Patrick Lencioni
The Five Dysfunctions of a Team

Trust is the foundation everything else sits on. Explains exactly why we treat trust, not process, as the multiplier on a small team.

Kent Beck
Extreme Programming Explained

Small releases, pairing, simple design, courage. Most of part two is XP values under a different name.

John Ousterhout
A Philosophy of Software Design

A rigorous case for "obvious beats short": complexity is the enemy, and it accumulates in small decisions, not big ones.

Robert C. Martin
Clean Architecture

The reasoning behind thin controllers and slices that own their own logic: dependencies should point at the business rules, not away from them.
